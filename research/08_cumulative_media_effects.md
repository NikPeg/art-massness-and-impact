# Cumulative media effects and event-centered measurement

Targeted search and appraisal, **9 November 2026**. The question is whether an event-centered definition misses changes in everyday affect, self-evaluation, habits, belonging and perceived norms. The studies below support measuring these outcomes separately. They do not establish a common magnitude of life impact across the eight media.

Distinguish four claims: an immediate response; repeated responses during exposure; change in a person's usual level or trajectory; and persistence after exposure changes or ends. Evidence for one claim does not automatically establish the next. Recurrent relief or distress can matter during exposure even if there is no lasting baseline shift. “Cumulative” is not assumed to mean a linear sum of effects or an irreversible change.

## Which project categories the evidence concerns

Classify evidence by the content or activity studied, not by the platform's name. Instagram as a delivery setting does not turn an image-exposure experiment into evidence about all social media, mixed feeds or short video.

## Online images: a trajectory experiment, IMGDAILY23

Fioravanti, Svicher, Ceragioli, Bruni and Casale, *Examining the impact of daily exposure to body-positive and fitspiration Instagram content on young women's mood and body image* (online 2021; journal issue 2023), randomly assigned 122 Italian women to three image conditions over 28 days. Body-positive exposure showed greater positive-mood growth than the alternatives and greater body-satisfaction growth than fitspiration. The detailed results did not establish greater body-satisfaction growth than neutral under the authors' evaluation; negative-mood growth did not differ between fitspiration and neutral. Thus the abstract's broad contrast needs qualification. Confidence is moderate for bounded during-exposure trajectories: self-reports, a narrow sample, imperfect model fit, and no post-exposure persistence test limit inference. Assign this evidence to **pictures**, specifically the selected image content; Instagram is the delivery setting. It does not establish effects of all pictures, whole social networks, mixed feeds or short video, and artistic status is not isolated. [Publisher](https://doi.org/10.1177/14614448211038904); [published full text, university-hosted copy](https://internet.psych.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/532-Master/532-UnitPages/Unit-07/Fioravanti_NMS_2023.pdf).

## Music: repeated regulation without demonstrated accumulation, MUSICREG23

Randall, Baltazar and Saarikallio, *Success in reaching affect self-regulation goals through everyday music listening* (online 2023; volume labeled 2022), used MuPsych with 293 Finnish participants. During one week, reports at listening onset and five minutes later assessed whether emotional goals were met. Success occurred in fewer than half of goal-directed episodes and depended on context and initial affect. This supports measuring everyday regulation, including unsuccessful attempts, without demanding a biographical event. It does not demonstrate a lasting baseline change, isolate streaming from downloaded playback, or test music embedded in videos. Confidence is moderate for reported episode-level patterns and insufficient for cumulative causal change. Publisher abstract and indexed methods/results were inspected; direct full-text/PDF retrieval returned 403. [Original article](https://doi.org/10.1080/09298215.2023.2187310).

The existing four-week virtual-gallery study, **PICART24**, is another relevant repeated-exposure design. Its appraisal remains in the [pictures review](02_literature_review.md#curated-online-viewing-picart24). Neither curated viewing nor deliberate music regulation can simply be treated as an ordinary mixed-feed experience.

## Self-esteem, affect and friendship: repeated observations, SMDAILY26

Van der Wal, Beyens, Janssen and Valkenburg, *Diverse platforms, diverse effects* (online December 2025; issue 2026), analyzed 44,211 daily diaries from 479 adolescents over 100 days. It reports person- and platform-specific associations across well-being, self-esteem and friendship closeness, including differing directions across outcomes within individuals. This provides a directly online example of separating several dimensions instead of a single impact score. Confidence is moderate for descriptive patterns within the sample, low for causal attribution. The authors explicitly describe the study as descriptive. Repeated diaries and person-specific modeling do not themselves identify cumulative dose effects or persistence after stopping. Platform use mixes creative content, messaging and other activity. [Publisher full text](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12144-025-08893-7).

## Daily versus monthly stress, SMSTRESS26

Siebers, Beyens, Keijsers, Valkenburg and van der Wal, *From Days to Months: The Effect of Social Media Use on Stress Among Adolescents Accumulates Over Time* (online 30 September 2026), analyzes the same-sized 100-day diary dataset: 479 adolescents and 44,211 observations. Reported average coefficients rise from .03 daily to .05 weekly, .07 biweekly and .13 monthly. This is suggestive evidence that results depend on timescale, not proof of an accumulating causal dose: observational associations and temporal aggregation leave alternative explanations. Confidence is low for causal accumulation. It should not be counted as an independent sample replication of SMDAILY26 without evidence of different recruitment. Only the original indexed abstract was appraised; publisher full text was unavailable. [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42820885/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jadohealth.2026.07.021).

## Belonging-related outcomes: an important null result, SMCLOSE21

Pouwels and colleagues, *Some socially poor but also some socially rich adolescents feel closer to their friends after using social media* (2021), combined three weeks of experience sampling with seven biweekly follow-ups in 383 adolescents. Short-term friendship-closeness associations varied between individuals. Across three months, average closeness did not change significantly, and short-term associations did not predict longer-term growth. Confidence is moderate for this bounded observational result; it neither proves no individual effects nor establishes causal accumulation. Friendship closeness is related to belonging but is not equivalent to cultural identity or community membership. Messaging and mixed-platform activity also differ from creative-content consumption. [Original full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8551228/).

## Habit mechanisms: adjacent online evidence, SMHABIT23

Ceylan, Anderson and Wood, *Sharing of misinformation is habitual, not just lazy or biased* (2023), combine four studies with 2,476 participants. In the reward-training experiment, sharing patterns persisted in test trials after rewards ended. This supports a mechanism through which repeated platform behavior can become habitual, with a behavioral outcome beyond an intention. Confidence is moderate for the bounded experimental mechanism, insufficient for durable everyday habit change caused by art. The outcome is news sharing in an experimental task, not creative viewing or an established long-term life habit. [Original full text](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2216614120).

## Perceived norms: contextual evidence outside the online target, NORMRAD09

Paluck, *Reducing Intergroup Prejudice and Conflict Using the Media: A Field Experiment in Rwanda* (2009), studied a yearlong radio soap-opera intervention against a health-program control. Perceived norms and some behaviors changed while personal beliefs changed little. Confidence is moderate for this setting and design. It supports distinguishing perceived norms, personal endorsement and behavior. Radio delivery, organized listening and a specific reconciliation intervention prevent treating this as an online-content effect estimate or an online category's score anchor. This is methodological context, explicitly outside the distribution target. [Original published paper, university-hosted copy](https://sparq.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj19021/files/media/file/paluck_2009_-_reducing_intergroup_prejudice_and_conflict_using_the_media.pdf).

## Implications and remaining gaps

The event-centered objection is supported as a measurement criticism: directly relevant online studies investigate changes without requiring a move, job change or relationship decision. Evidence is strongest here for bounded content-specific trajectories; ordinary creative-feed accumulation, durable music-related identity change and long-term everyday habit formation remain incompletely identified. Null and mixed results rule out assuming that every repeated reaction accumulates in the same direction.

Report two outcome families separately: **major events** and **everyday/cumulative changes**. Within the second family, report episode-level burden or benefit, during-exposure trajectories, and post-exposure persistence separately. A large life event is not required for the second family to count as impact. Sign, magnitude, duration and personal importance are distinct variables; small recurrent effects can be relevant without a clinically significant change or a declared turning point.

For online music embedded in short vertical videos, long horizontal videos, films, series and games, retain both primary-format and musical-component tags. Its presence is substantive, including incidental audible contact. These sources do not isolate the soundtrack's contribution from the host work, so they cannot attribute an audiovisual effect wholly to either music or video.

This is a targeted review, not a systematic search or pooled estimate. Studies vary in population, outcome and timescale; the two recent diary papers are not treated as independent replications.
